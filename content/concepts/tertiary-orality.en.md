Walter Ong described a primary orality that preceded writing, and a literacy that displaced it. He added a secondary orality, carried by radio and television, which reproduced the intimacy of speech while remaining anchored in a literate order — its scripts were written, its speakers composed.

A third condition has now arrived, and it is not a variation on the second. Large language models emit utterances bearing every mark of composition: register, cadence, argumentative shape, the appearance of having been meant. Behind them stands no one who meant anything. The utterance has a form without having an origin.

This matters because the whole apparatus of literate trust runs on the assumption that a text implies an author, and that an author can be held to it. Citation, attribution, plagiarism, authority, liability, the byline itself — each is a device for connecting words to a person who can be asked about them. Tertiary orality severs that connection while leaving every one of its outward signs intact.

The consequence is that our procedures for evaluating a claim now run on a signal that has been decoupled from what it once indicated. We read fluency as evidence of thought, coherence as evidence of care, and grammatical confidence as evidence of a mind that has checked something. Those inferences were reliable for as long as producing fluent text was expensive. They are now free.
