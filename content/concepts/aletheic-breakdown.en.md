A polity can survive a great deal of disagreement about the facts. What it cannot survive is the loss of an agreed method for settling the disagreement. Aletheic breakdown describes the second condition, and it is the more dangerous one, because it is invisible while the arguments continue in their usual form.

Verification once had a cost, and that cost was doing quiet work. Printing was expensive, distribution was slow, retraction was public and humiliating, and the interval between a claim and its publication was long enough for someone to check. None of this was designed as an epistemic safeguard. It was friction, and it functioned as one anyway.

Removing the friction removed the safeguard. Here is the part that most reform proposals miss: the safeguard was never in anyone's stated intentions, so nobody thought to replace it when the infrastructure changed.

I therefore treat friction as a democratic resource rather than an inefficiency. This is an unfashionable position in a field organised around speed, reach and seamlessness, and I think it is correct.
